## Slide 2

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 2]
Our data is the Digital Library of India collection on the Internet Archive: public scans, no API key needed. A fleet of Google Cloud machines harvested 15,288 books, and Tesseract read five pages of each to measure OCR quality.

A book is usable only if its mean OCR confidence is at least 50 and it has at least 100 clean tokens. 13,342 books pass, which is 87.3%. Per language: English 1,970 of 2,000; Bengali 1,927 of 2,000; Gujarati 1,883 of 2,000; Hindi 1,804 of 2,000; Sanskrit 1,562 of 2,000; Kannada 1,459 of 1,608; Tamil 1,416 of 1,766; Telugu 1,321 of 1,914. The excluded books stay in the database, flagged, not silently dropped.

In the variable audit we dropped scanning centre and source library, because groups could form by scanning site rather than topic. Year is filled with the median and a missing flag.

Usable books are split train, validation and test by a hash of each book's identifier, so a book never changes split as the corpus grows: 8,005, 2,668 and 2,669.

## Slide 3

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 3]
Figure 3.1 shows the preprocessing. Tesseract reads each page; when its script guess disagrees with the catalogue, both language models read the page and the more confident reading wins. For 0 books we use the archive's own whole-book OCR as the training text; our five Tesseract pages measure quality.

Text is normalised to Unicode NFC and cleaned per script. Each book is turned into a vector with Google's Vertex AI text-multilingual-embedding-002: up to ten 64-word chunks, averaged.

Multilingual vectors cluster by language. On the training books, without correction, 0% of each book's nearest neighbours share its script, and script NMI of the groups is 0.000. Subtracting each language's average vector brings these to 0% and 0.000. PCA then keeps 100 dimensions.

## Slide 4

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 4]
Thank you, Rohan. Figure 4.1 compares the four linkages on all 13,342 books. Cophenetic correlation measures how well each tree preserves the original distances: average 0.485, complete 0.398, Ward 0.396, single 0.036. Single linkage shows the classic chaining: books join one at a time, so it cannot give topic groups.

We did not pick k by eye. The grid covers Ward, average and complete linkage for k from 2 to 15, and the setting is chosen on the training books only by a rule we wrote down before the results: script NMI ≤ 0.5 and bootstrap ARI ≥ 0.5, then highest silhouette; if none passes, lowest script NMI (reported as a rule failure). The result is ward linkage with k = 3. No setting met the rule, so the pre-registered fallback applied; we report that openly.

## Slide 5

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 5]
Unit IV: DBSCAN. It needs a radius eps and a minimum neighbourhood size MinPts. The sorted k-distance curves in the bottom figure set the range of eps; we then ran 24 settings and kept the one with the best silhouette among valid ones: eps 0.866, MinPts 20. It finds 2 dense clusters and calls 42.4% of the books noise.

On the books DBSCAN does keep, its clusters and the hierarchical groups agree with an adjusted Rand index of -0.001. So in this embedding, density and topic are different structures: we use the hierarchical groups as the catalogue and show DBSCAN's verdict alongside.

## Slide 6

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 6]
Course Outcome CO3. PCA is used twice. First, it reduces the 768-dimensional embeddings to 100 dimensions, keeping 78.0% of the variance; clustering happens there. Second, for display, it projects those vectors to 2D and 3D: PC1 11.0%, PC2 8.5%, PC3 5.6%.

19.5% in two dimensions is low, and that is normal for text: topic differences are spread over many directions. So the plot shows overlap that does not exist in the full space. We chose PCA over t-SNE or UMAP because it does not invent gaps between groups.

## Slide 7

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 7]
Figure 7.1 shows silhouette across k for each linkage. Our selection rule, written before any results, keeps settings whose groups are not just languages and that survive resampling, and then takes the best silhouette.

On the Vertex embeddings no setting survived resampling well enough, so the rule fell back to the setting least tied to script. The chosen model is ward linkage with k = 3: silhouette 0.040, script NMI 0.018, bootstrap ARI 0.24. A silhouette this close to zero means the groups overlap heavily; we judge them by held-out subject accuracy instead, which Sai Pranav presents next.

## Slide 9

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 9]
We judged the groups against targets written before the results. All fitting used the training books; validation and test books were only transformed, and the test numbers were measured once.

T2, subject: each group predicts the majority subject of its training books. On 118 labelled test books that is right 47.5% of the time, against 42.4% for the best simple guess, which is guessing from the script alone. That is +5.1 points; the target was +10, so it is missed. Shuffling the labels 2,000 times never matched it: p = 0.0005.

T1: a single scanned page lands in its own book's group 79.1% of the time; the target was 80%, missed. T3: the groups' bootstrap stability is 0.23 against a target of 0.60, missed.
For honesty: our earlier Gemini study on fewer books reached 58.7% subject accuracy, +10.7 points. More books with a different encoder did not give better topics.

## Slide 10

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 10]
Our limits. OCR quality varies by language: Telugu keeps only 69% of its books after the quality gate. The groups are broad and only partly stable, with bootstrap ARI 0.24. Our subject labels were made with an AI assistant for the team to check, so they are a measuring tool, not expert cataloguing. And the encoder is an online Google model.

So we present the groups as a navigation aid for archivists, not as a catalogue. The app abstains when a page has fewer than 20 readable words, warns when OCR is weak, and always shows its evidence: the nearest archive books and how close the page sits to its group.
