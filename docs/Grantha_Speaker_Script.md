## Slide 1: Grantha Heritage Tracker

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 1]

Good morning. We are Rohan, Rajith and Sai Pranav, and this is the Grantha Heritage Tracker, for Smart India Hackathon problem SIH26096.

The idea in one line: old printed Indian books sit in a digital library in many scripts, and we asked whether a computer can group them by subject without being told the subjects.

Grantha is a working web app built on that idea. Every number you will see comes from our result files. We set three targets before the results; where we missed one, we say so.

## Slide 2: The problem: about half of the books cannot be found by subject

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 2]

Start with the problem a real archive has.

The Digital Library of India holds scans of old printed books. Of the 13,358 books we could use, 6,825 carry a library class, the subject code a librarian gives a book. The rest have none, so a subject search cannot find them. They are scanned, but in practice they are lost.

Three things make this hard to fix by hand. One: books in different scripts never meet, so a Tamil book and a Telugu book on the same subject sit in separate piles. Two: a loose page with no cover or title cannot be filed at all. Three: cataloguing needs a person who can read each language, and there are far more books than such people.

So our question: can a computer read the books and group them by subject on its own, across scripts?

## Slide 3: Grantha is a web app that tells an archivist where a book belongs

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 3]

This is what we built: Grantha, a web app for the archivist. It is running live on a cloud server, and it does four things.

One, it places a page. The archivist uploads one scanned page, the app reads it, and shows the group it belongs to and the closest books in the archive. In our test a single page landed in its own book's group 75.2% of the time; a random guess manages 35.9%.

Two, it suggests a class for a book that has none, by letting the nearest classed books vote. On 1,168 test books, the book's own class was among the first three suggestions 91.6% of the time; the top suggestion alone was right 58.6%, against 47.8% for always naming the most common class.

Three, it links books across scripts. After our fix, 37.2% of a book's 10 closest books are in another script; before it, 2.7%.

Four, it draws the whole archive as a map, which you will see in a moment.

The site also lists scans that should be redone and looks for other editions of a book; both are early, and we say so on the limits slide. When a page has too few readable words, the app refuses to guess.

## Slide 4: Five steps from a scanned page to a subject group

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 4]

Here is the whole method in five steps.

One, read: OCR, software that turns a picture of a page into text. We downloaded 15,288 books in 8 languages, and 13,358 were read clearly enough to use.

Two, embed: an embedding is a list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a text, so books on similar topics get similar numbers. We use Vertex AI Multilingual embeddings, which reads all our languages.

Three, centre: we subtract each language's average list of numbers. Without this, the strongest signal is the language itself, not the subject; the next slide shows the measurement. This is our key step.

Four, group, in two ways. Hierarchical clustering joins the closest books step by step into a tree, and we cut the tree into groups. DBSCAN looks for crowded regions of books and marks the rest as noise.

Five, check: we test the groups on books we kept aside, and compare with simple guesses.

## Slide 5: Left alone, books group by language; centring removes it

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 5]

This is our main finding.

We take the 8,129 training books and group them twice with the same model: once with the numbers as the model gives them, and once after subtracting each language's average.

The top chart uses NMI, a score of how much the groups follow language: 0 means not at all, 1 means each group is exactly one language. As the model gives them, NMI is 0.64; after centring it is 0.07. So centring takes language out of the groups.

The bottom chart asks a simpler question: of each book's 10 closest books, how many are written in the same script? As given, 97%. After centring, 63%. Left alone, a book's neighbours are mostly books in its own language, whatever the subject.

On the left, for comparison: an earlier study that grouped books by counting words found one group per language.

## Slide 6: On the UMAP map, 7 of 8 languages mix; English books still stand apart

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 6]

This is the archive map from the app. Each dot is one book.

The layout is made by UMAP, a method that draws many-dimensional points on a flat page while keeping each book next to its closest neighbours. So nearby dots are books the model reads as similar. Distances between far-apart clumps mean little. It is drawn after centring, the step from the last slide.

Both panels show the same dots. On the left, the red dots are English books. The grey dots, every other language, share one large body, so those languages have mixed. The English books have not: they keep their own islands. Centring removes the average difference between languages, but a book's closest neighbours are still often in its own language, and a neighbour map shows exactly that. This matches the measurement: after centring, 37.2% of a book's closest books are in another script, up from 2.7%, which is a real change and still a minority. We have not tested why English stands apart, so we only report it.

On the right, the same map coloured by the 3 groups that hierarchical clustering found. The groups overlap on the map: they are broad, and the borders between them are soft.

The slide shows a sample of the 13,358 books in two dimensions. In the app the map is in 3-D with every book: you can turn it, switch the colour between language and group, and open sample books. A picture explains; the measurements prove.

## Slide 7: Hierarchical clustering finds 3 broad groups, each mixing all 8 languages

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 7]

Hierarchical clustering starts with every book alone, joins the two closest, then the next closest, and so on, until all the books hang on one tree. We cut the tree into 3 groups, using ward linkage; the number of groups was chosen on the training books by a rule we fixed beforehand.

Each row is a group. The columns show what share of the group comes from each language: the darker the cell, the bigger the share. No language holds more than 22% of any group, so the groups are not language piles.

The subject column is the most common subject among the hand-labelled training books in that group: 2 groups lean to religion and philosophy; one group leans to literature. These are broad shelves, not a catalogue.

How good are they? On 117 labelled books kept aside, each group predicts its most common subject and is right 53.0% of the time. The best simple guess, guessing from the script alone, is right 38.5%. So the groups lead by 14.5 points against a target of +10.0: met. The likely range is +5.1 to +24.8 points, and the validation books gave +4.2, so we hold this result with care. Random shuffles of the labels almost never give a gain this big.

One honest number: the silhouette, a score of how cleanly groups separate, from 0 for overlapping to 1 for clean, is 0.04. The groups overlap a great deal.

## Slide 8: DBSCAN finds 2 crowded regions and calls 31% of the books noise

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 8]

Our second method, DBSCAN, asks a different question. It does not cut the books into shelves. It looks for crowded regions, places where many books sit close together, and calls every book outside them noise.

It finds 2 crowded regions and marks 30.7% of the books as noise. Noise here is not an error: it means the book has no close crowd, so any group we give it deserves less trust. The app shows this verdict beside every answer.

The chart shows how steady each method is. We re-ran each one 20 times on a random part of the books and compared the result with the full run, using ARI, a score of how much two groupings agree: 1 is identical, 0 is chance.

The grey dots are DBSCAN: around 0.86, very steady. The red dots are the hierarchical groups: the middle value is 0.26, against our target of 0.6. We did not meet it: the hierarchical groups shift when the books change.

The two methods also barely agree with each other: their ARI is 0.00. So we use them for different jobs. Hierarchical clustering gives every book a shelf; DBSCAN says how sure we can be.

## Slide 9: We met 1 of our 3 targets; here is what it does not do yet

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 9]

Our results and limits, plainly.

We set three targets before seeing any results, and this study met 1 of them. A page lands with its book 75.2% of the time against a target of 80%: not met. The subject gain is +14.5 points against +10.0: met, though the validation books gave +4.2. Stability, measured on the training books, is 0.36 against 0.6: not met.

Against the librarians' own catalogue classes, rather than our subject labels, the groups score -4.0 points from the best simple guess, so they do not beat it there. The score behind the list of scans to redo separates good and bad scans with an AUC of 0.50, where 0.5 is chance, so it is no better than chance. The edition finder made no false report but found 36.4% of known editions, in only 11 test groups, so that number is very uncertain.

The groups are broad: 3 groups for a whole library. Hard-to-read languages are under-represented; Telugu keeps only 69% of its books. The subject labels we test with were made with an AI assistant and checked by us; they are a measuring tool, not expert cataloguing. And the embedding model is an online Google service, so checking a page sends its text to Google.

So we offer this as a helper that shows its evidence, not as a catalogue.

## Slide 10: Take away the language, and subject starts to show

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 10]

To sum up in three sentences.

One: books group by language first; subtracting each language's average lets subject come through.

Two: the groups beat simple guesses and reach the subject target on test books: +14.5 points on test books against +10.0; validation gave +4.2; not yet stable.

Three: Grantha is a working app that helps an archivist today: it places a new page, suggests a class for an uncatalogued book, links books across scripts, and draws the whole archive as one map, always with its evidence.

Thank you. We are happy to take your questions.
