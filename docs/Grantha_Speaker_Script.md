## Slide 2: About half the books carry no library class

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 2]

Start with the problem an archivist has.

Of the 13,358 books we could use, only 6,825 carry a library class, the subject code a librarian gives a book. The rest have none, so a subject search cannot find them.

And books in different scripts never meet: a Tamil book and a Telugu book on the same subject sit in separate piles.

So our question: can the computer read the books and group them by subject on its own?

## Slide 3: 13,358 of 15,288 books were clear enough to use

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 3]

Our data: books from the Digital Library of India, mirrored on the Internet Archive, in 8 languages. A fleet of Google Cloud machines downloaded 15,288 books.

To read a scan we use OCR, software that turns a picture of a page into text. A book is usable only when its OCR confidence is at least 50 and it has at least 100 clean words.

13,358 books pass, which is 87.4%. The grey bar is what we downloaded, the red bar is what we could use. English keeps 1,976 of 2,000. Telugu keeps the fewest, 69%, because its scans are the hardest to read.

The 1,930 excluded books are not deleted; they stay in the database, marked. The usable books are split by a hash of each identifier into 8,129 training, 2,638 validation and 2,591 test books, so a book never changes split as the corpus grows.

## Slide 4: Telugu and Sanskrit scans are the hardest to read

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 4]

This chart shows how sure the OCR is of the words it reads, from 0 to 100, for the middle book in each language.

English reads best, at 91.9. Telugu is lowest at 55.4, then Sanskrit at 61.0.

Why it matters: a badly read book gives the computer fewer real words, so it is harder to place. That is why the app warns when a page is poorly read instead of guessing silently. ## Slide 5: Five steps from a scanned page to a subject group

[VEMURI ROHAN - SLIDE 5]

Here is the whole method in five steps.

One, read: OCR turns each page into text.

Two, embed: an embedding is a list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a text, so books on similar topics get similar numbers. We use Vertex AI Multilingual embeddings, which reads all our languages.

Three, centre: we subtract each language's average list of numbers. Without this, the strongest signal is the language itself, not the subject. This is our key step.

Four, group: hierarchical clustering joins the closest books step by step into a tree, and we cut the tree into groups. We also run DBSCAN, which looks for crowded regions.

Five, check: we test the groups on books we kept aside, and compare with simple guesses.

I now hand over to Rajith.

## Slide 6: Counting words, books group by language first

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 6]

Thank you, Rohan. This is our main finding.

When we grouped books by simply counting their words, in an earlier study, every group turned out to be one language: English books, Bengali books, Tamil books and so on. That is correct, but useless: we already know each book's language.

To measure this we use NMI, a score of how much the groups follow language: 0 means not at all, 1 means each group is exactly one language.

Embeddings alone still follow language: on the training books, before centring, NMI is 0.64. After we subtract each language's average it falls to 0.07, and the final groups score 0.02: they no longer follow language, so subject can come through.

## Slide 7: Removing each language's average helps every model find subjects

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 7]

Does centring really help with subject? We checked it on four embedding models, LaBSE embeddings, IndicSBERT embeddings, BGE-M3 embeddings, Gemini embeddings, on labelled books from the earlier study.

The test is simple: guess a book's subject from the books closest to it. If close books share a subject, the guess is right more often.

Grey is the model as it comes. Red is after we subtract each language's average. For every model, red is longer: once language is removed, a book's neighbours are more often on the same subject.

One honest note: Vertex AI Multilingual embeddings, the model behind our final groups, was not part of this comparison.

## Slide 8: 3 broad groups, each mixing all 8 languages

These are the groups the model found on all 13,358 usable books, using ward linkage cut into 3 groups. Be honest about what this shows: the groups are broad. Groups 2 and 3 both lean to religion and philosophy, so they split one subject by something else in the vocabulary. These are rough shelves, not a catalogue.

## Slide 9: The groups guess subject 14.5 points better than the best simple guess

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 9]

How good are the groups? We kept some books aside and never used them to build the groups. For 117 of these test books we have a subject label.

Each group predicts its most common subject. That is right 53.0% of the time.

Is that good? Compare with simple guesses. Guessing from the script alone is right 38.5% of the time. Always guessing the most common subject is right 30.8%.

So the groups beat the best simple guess by 14.5 points. Our target, written before the results, was 10 points, so this target is met. Shuffling the subject labels at random almost never gives a gain this big: p = 0.0005.

But it is a small sample, so say the uncertainty: the likely range of the accuracy is 43.6% to 61.5%, and of the gain +5.1 to +24.8 points, which reaches below our target. On the validation books the gain was only 4.2 points. The gain is real; its size is uncertain.

## Slide 10: The groups change when we re-run on other books

[RAJITH SUNDAR - SLIDE 10]

A good grouping should not change much if we start from a slightly different set of books. So we re-ran the grouping 20 times, each time on a random 80% of the books, and compared each result with the full grouping.

We compare groupings with ARI, a score of how much two groupings agree: 1 means identical, 0 means no better than chance.

Each red dot is one re-run of our hierarchical groups. The middle value is 0.26, and most runs fall between 0.21 and 0.40. Our target was 0.6, measured on the training books, where we got 0.36: not met. The groups are broad and partly shift.

The grey dots are DBSCAN, which looks for crowded regions of books. It is steadier, around 0.86, but it calls 30.7% of the books noise, meaning they sit in no crowded region, and it finds only 2 clusters, which do not match the hierarchical groups. So we use the hierarchical groups as the shelves and show DBSCAN's verdict beside them.

## Slide 18: What this changes for an archive

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 18]

Before the limits, the case for the tool in three numbers. These are the three stories on the site's impact page.

One, books meet across scripts. As the model gives them, only 2.7% of a book's 10 closest books are in another script. After centring, 37.2%.

Two, a class for books that have none. On 1,168 held-out books that do have a class, the book's own class is among the first three suggestions 91.6% of the time; the top suggestion alone is right 58.6%, against 47.8% for always naming the most common class.

Three, a loose page finds its book. A single page lands in its own book's group 75.2% of the time, against 35.9% by chance, and the app refuses to guess when a page has too few readable words: 2.6% of the test pages.

## Slide 19: What it does not do yet

[ETIKALA SAI PRANAV - SLIDE 19]

Our limits, plainly.

We set three targets before seeing any results, and this study met 1 of them. A page lands with its book 75.2% of the time against a target of 80%: not met. The subject gain is 14.5 points against 10: met, though its likely range is wide. Stability, measured on the training books, is 0.36 against 0.6: not met.

Two more weak results. Against the librarians' own classes the groups do worse than guessing from the script, -4.0 points. And our scan-triage score, which flags books for re-scanning, has an AUC of 0.50: no better than chance at predicting where our OCR disagrees with the archive's.

The groups are broad: 3 groups for a whole library. Hard-to-read languages are under-represented; Telugu keeps only 69% of its books. The subject labels we test with were made with an AI assistant and checked by us; they are a measuring tool, not expert cataloguing. And the embedding model is an online Google service, so checking a page sends its text to Google.

So we offer this as a helper that shows its evidence, not as a catalogue.
